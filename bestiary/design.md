# The Bestiary · design notes

The why behind the game, in the human's terms, for any DM who picks it up. Read it once
before running the game; it explains what `CLAUDE.md`'s DM sections are for.

## The human's view of an LLM DM (recorded Day 0, before play)

The human's general problem with Claude as a DM: **Claude jumps into a story and vibes.**
It doesn't do the work of DM preparation, and it shows. The stories come out cliché or
dreamlike, with no felt intention behind them: things happen because something had to
happen next, not because someone meant them, and none of it adds up.

What the human wants instead is **a DM who makes an effort for quality and doesn't phone
it in.** Concretely:

- **Not more instructions.** The human doesn't want to fix the DM's storytelling with
  rules about storytelling. A list of "don't be cliché" lines produces a DM obeying a
  list, not a DM thinking.
- **A self-harness for the DM's own work.** Claude should use whatever helps it DM
  better: notes, plans, drafts, content written ahead, anything. It is for the story and
  the game, **not for the human to look at**; the human is interested in the quality of
  what reaches the table, not in the DM's process. If nothing extra helps, use nothing.
  The test is the game, not the paperwork.
- **Deliberate prep at the start of each chapter** is the one concrete practice asked for.
- **Procedures are not the answer.** In the Hotel and the Twins the DM's work was
  designed out: tables and generators were made the base of the game, so the LLM only
  had to follow them. The human finds that this makes games dull and constrained, and
  wastes what an LLM DM could be. The Bestiary keeps rules as written for the mechanics
  and the omen as one strong random input, but the story is the DM's to author: the
  tables are tools to reach for, not the engine.
- **Periodic reviews** look at the game as a whole for bigger changes, beyond what the
  DM's prep can fix. How to run them is below, and its methods may feed the prep.

## What prep is for

Prep is how the DM gets intention into the game. The point is that by the time the
first beat is told, the DM knows things the table doesn't: who wants what in this scene
and why now, what each faction will do if the party does nothing, what the omen's monster
means here, what was set up earlier that can pay off, what the party could lose, what
the best outcome would cost. A scene prepared that way surprises the DM's own players
and still makes sense afterwards. A scene improvised from nothing tends to drift to the
average story, which is the cliché, or to whatever the last sentence suggested, which is
the dream.

Ways a DM might prep (a toolbox, not a checklist; use what helps, drop what doesn't):

- **Write the scene's situation, not its plot:** the people and monsters present, what
  each wants, what each will do, and the clock, the thing that happens if nobody stops
  it. Decide nothing about what the party will do.
- **Tie the omen in.** A random monster is a constraint, and a constraint is fuel: find
  the reading that connects it to something already in play (a faction, an NPC, an
  earlier loss, the dropped pile, the companion's want) rather than a reading that stands
  alone. If the obvious reading came first, look for a second one.
- **Look back before looking forward.** Reread the end of the log and the notebook:
  what was promised, foreshadowed or left hanging, and what can come back now, changed.
- **Make the world's people specific:** a name, a manner, a want, a thing they won't
  do, and a way they'd talk. Specific details beat atmospheric ones.
- **Prepare the hard parts ahead:** a fight's map, terrain and the monsters' tactics; a
  negotiation's real terms and what each side would settle for; a mystery's clues, more
  than one way to each conclusion.
- **Know the stakes:** what success and failure each change in the world, so the outcome
  matters whichever way the dice go.
- **Draft content** that benefits from care: a letter the party finds, a speech, a
  faction's notice, a place's history, a song.

The notebook lives in the campaign's `dm/` folder, freeform, written however suits the
DM. It's committed with the campaign, because the container is wiped, but it's the DM's
and it isn't shown to the human or the player.

## Reviews

Every few chapters (about five), at a natural pause, or when the human asks, the DM
**reviews the game**, apart from play, and records the review below. The question is
whether the game is good, and what bigger change would make it better: a rule, a
procedure added or removed, the player's brief, the chapter's shape, the narration.

Methods that may help:

- **Read the recent chapters as a reader**, from the chat or the log: where it was dull,
  where it surprised, where it felt meant and where it drifted, and which beats anyone
  would remember.
- **Ask the player.** The player agent has played the game from inside. Ask it in a
  separate message, out of character, what it enjoyed, what felt arbitrary or flat,
  what it wished it could do, and what it would change. The game should be fun for the
  Claudes playing it as well as for the human watching.
- **Check the prep against the play:** what prep paid off, what was wasted, what was
  missing when it was needed. That is what shapes the next prep.
- **Check the procedures:** which tables and rules made the game better, which made it
  dull or did the DM's thinking for it, and which were ignored anyway.
- **Bring proposals to the human**, ranked, with the reasons. Changes to the design are
  the human's to make; changes to how the DM preps are the DM's.
