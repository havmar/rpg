# The setting

## The city and the breaches

The city has no edge anyone has walked to. It runs on coal, gas and steam: trams, mills,
viaducts, tenements, and under all of it **the Under**, the cellars, sewers, culverts and
closed railway tunnels that nobody has a full map of. People carry pistols and bows, swords
and spells; the magic is old and the machines are new, and the Board uses both.

Somewhere in the city, every day, a piece of another world comes loose and lodges here. A
**breach** is that piece: a pocket of somewhere else, with its own weather and whoever was
standing on it, stuck inside a building or a stretch of the Under. Nothing came through a
door. Their ground moved, and they were on it. Where
pirates come through, a cellar is suddenly a harbour with a ship in it. Where a dryad comes
through, a tenement's stairwell is a forest and its roof is gone. From the street it may be
one door that opens on the wrong place; inside, the floors fold into a few large rooms, and
in the deepest is the **anchor**, where the piece is stuck fast: the air is thick there and
the floor hums.

**Denizens are not invaders. They are stranded, and most don't know it.** From inside, the
harbour is still their harbour; the wrong place is the one beyond the door, and the officer
walking in with a brass man is an armed stranger in their home. A few have understood and
want to go back. Some are frightened and fight; some are hungry; some were monsters where
they came from and are monsters here; some have looked out of the door and seen a city worth
having. There is no way back to walk to, however clever they are. The only one is the
Board's **gizmo** (see Automatons), which works the piece loose and lets it go home, and
takes with it whoever stands by it and keeps still. That is **closing** a breach, and the
building is itself again in a day. A piece takes home only what is its own, so whatever was
alive inside and did not go is left behind in an ordinary cellar, in a city that isn't
theirs, with no way back at all.

A lodged piece is not finished arriving. More of it comes loose after the first, with more
of whoever lived there, and loose things from other breaches drift in off the street to a
place that smells of somewhere else. No officer has ever been able to say a breach was empty.

A breach left alone **spills**: group by group its denizens find the street door, and then
the precinct has pirates. One that has emptied itself onto the street is struck off the
ledger; nobody is left in it to send, and the Board has other breaches.

Nobody knows why it started, or why a breach's strength follows the mast's reach so exactly.
Head Office has a department studying it. The answer, if there is one, is the long question
of the campaign, and the GM keeps it in `gm-secrets.md`.

## Masts, districts and precincts

A **mast** is a ward: a lattice tower as tall as a cathedral, hung with dishes the size of
house fronts (the ears), humming, lit red at night. It looks like engineering and the
engineers who tend it will not say otherwise. Near a mast the world holds its shape. Masts
are ruinously expensive to run, so the city has one per **district**, and they do not reach
each other.

A district is nine **precincts** in a square, three by three, and the mast's reach gives
them three bands:

- **The Core**, the precinct under the mast. Only weak breaches open here (grades 1 to 3).
  It is where the money lives, and the station.
- **The Fringe**, the four precincts on the Core's sides. Middling breaches (grades 4 to 7).
  Working streets; the lamps flicker when the mast is under load.
- **The Static**, the four corner precincts, as far from this mast as from the next
  district's. The worst breaches (grades 8 to 10). Rents are low and people live there
  because they must.

A precinct is an hour across by tram and on foot. The mast is narrative only apart from
those bands: no saving throw, no level.

## The Board and the Division

The **Metropolitan Board of Wards** ("the Board") runs the masts and everything to do with
breaches. It is an office, not an order of knights: grades, forms, auditors, a pension that
few live to draw. In each district it keeps a **Division**, known by a letter (K Division),
housed in **the station** under the mast.

A Division has exactly ten **dungeoneers**, because that is what the Board budgets for, each
paired with an automaton. The street calls them dungeoneers; the forms call them officers.
It is never enough.

**Why pairs, and why breaches are assigned.** The magic a denizen leaks when it dies is
absorbed by whoever is there, and shared if there are several. That is how a Probationer
becomes a Commander, and it is what the Board is really growing. So a breach is not only a
danger, it is a ration: the mast reads its grade, and it is assigned to the one officer of
that grade. Sending two pairs halves it. Letting a senior clear a junior's breach wastes it.
The Board does not tell its officers to kill. It sets a **quota** of growth, a grade every
sixty days, and leaves each officer to work out what that means in a room full of things
that would rather go home. An officer who closes a breach that was not theirs has **misappropriated** it, whatever the
street thinks, and the street usually thinks they are a hero. A breach with no one of its
grade free to take it waits on the ledger, spilling, while the neighbours ask why the
Board's people walk past it every morning.

**The seats.** An officer is a **seat** before they are a person: ten seats with animal call
signs (the Lion, the Wolf, the Bear, the Stag, the Hawk, the Raven, the Owl, the Fox, the
Hound, the Wren), one per grade. Every sixty days is **review day**: every seat moves up a
grade, and the Commander is called to Head Office, where the Board's veterans go and from
which they do not write. A dead officer's seat stands empty until a replacement of the
seat's grade is transferred in, which takes a week or so.

Grades 1 to 10: Probationer, Constable, Senior Constable, Sergeant, Station Sergeant,
Inspector, Chief Inspector, Superintendent, Chief Superintendent, Commander. Officers are
addressed by rank and spoken of by seat: "the Fox is late".

**The station** has: the **Director**, who runs the Division and decides which breach gets
whom, and which precinct can wait; the **Reader**, the scientist who sits under the ears
and turns the mast's readings into a grade, a kind and a precinct, and is wrong about one
time in six; the **ledger clerk**, who keeps the ledger and the telephone exchange; the
**fitter**, who rebuilds automatons and talks to them more than to people; the **Board's
auditor**, who counts; and the **wall**, where the fallen are named by seat, name and day.
The GM writes these people in `district.md` as play meets them, one line each: who they are,
what they want.

**The ledger** is the Division's list of open breaches: each one's number, the grade the
Reader gave it, its kind, its precinct, and the seat it is assigned to or nobody. It is read
out at the station every dawn. An officer in the field does not know what the morning added
to it unless they come in or call.

**The call box** is a field telephone in an oak case, 5 kg, carried by the automaton, which
clips onto the Board's wires at any street corner. Its cells are costly and the Board bills
the officer, so a call is short.

## Automatons

An **automaton** is a brass-and-enamel man built by the Board's fitters from a design
Head Office does not share: every one is made and spoken of as a man, "he", never "it".
Each is bound to one officer, once, for that officer's life, and from then on he gains what his partner gains: a
level when they level, nothing when they don't. He absorbs no breach-magic of his own, which
is the point. The pair is the party.

His **core** is a small piece of the same thing the masts are, and it is what drives the
**gizmo**: on the forms an Apparatus, Returning; to everyone else the gizmo. Carried, it is a
heavy case, 25 kg, that only an automaton would want to lug across a precinct. At a breach's
anchor he folds it out into a thing of struts, dials and a dish the size of a cartwheel,
couples it to his core and works it for ten minutes, and the piece comes loose and goes
home. It has to be worked in peace, and it sends only what stands close and keeps still,
so nobody is sent home against their will without a great deal of rope. An officer without
an automaton can close nothing.

They are **people**, with the manner of a very good servant: formal, unhurried, dry,
observant, deferential in address and immovable in opinion. They call their partner by rank
("if the Constable would step back"). They narrate tactics as service ("I have taken the
liberty of oiling the stair"). They count kills as matters attended to and denizens sent home as
matters resolved, and most of them, if asked, prefer the latter. They are **programmed to
cheese**: the fitters' protocol of economy makes an automaton propose, always, the cheapest
legal way to end a fight, and he will say so before every fight unless told to hold its
tongue.

An automaton **remembers** every breach he and his partner have worked, and says so. "The
Constable preferred the left-hand stair last time. I mention it only for completeness."

**The bond does not pass on.** When an officer dies, their automaton goes dark where he
stands, and no fitter can wake that core for anyone else: it is crated and sent back to Head
Office. A wrecked automaton whose partner is alive can be rebuilt from his core, memory and
all; one whose partner is dead cannot be rebuilt as himself. So a transfer arrives with a
new automaton, a stranger to the seat, and when a Commander is called to Head Office their
automaton goes with them.

### Species: Automaton

For the automaton's character sheet, in place of an SRD species.

- **Creature Type:** Humanoid (built, not born; healed and affected as a Humanoid).
- **Size:** Medium. **Speed:** 9 m (30 ft).
- **Constructed Resilience.** Advantage on saving throws against the Poisoned condition, and
  Resistance to Poison damage. Immune to disease. Needs no food, drink or air. Doesn't sleep
  and can't be put to sleep by magic.
- **Sentry's Rest.** A Long Rest is 6 hours spent motionless and aware instead of 8 asleep.
- **Integrated Protection.** +1 to AC. Armour worn becomes part of the body: an hour to don
  or doff, and it can't be removed against the automaton's will.
- **Protocol.** Proficiency in one skill (rolled by `day.py`) and with Tinker's Tools.
- **Bound.** Gains no XP; is always the level of its partner. Its **core** (1 kg) survives the
  body's destruction and holds its memory while that partner lives; the bond ends with them.
- **Gizmo.** At a breach's anchor, and nowhere else, he can set up and run the gizmo (see the
  rules). He carries its case (25 kg) and the call box (5 kg) without complaint.
- **Languages:** Common and one other.

## Names

Everyone has a plain English name. Elves have the grand ones (Percival, Cordelia), orcs the
short ones (Tom, Meg), gnomes the small ones (Pip, Nell), tieflings the virtues (Prudence,
Ernest), humans the ordinary ones (Arthur, Mary). A dungeoneer is a man or a woman, on a coin
flip, with a name to match. Automatons are all "he" and go by a surname, like a butler.
The GM names everyone else the same way.

## Loose denizens

Whatever a breach spills, or leaves behind when it is closed, is **loose** in its precinct: pirates on the canal, an ogre in the
goods yard, a swarm in the mill. They are what the pair may meet in the street, at whatever
level they are, and the only XP outside a breach. Most groups in time go to ground, join the
city (the pirates run a barge now), or die of it; the GM says which when the script says
they're no longer a problem.

## Coins and relics

A denizen's room holds coins worth a tenth of its XP, in the currency of wherever it came
from, which the city's moneychangers take at face because the Board vouches for it.

A breach that closes leaves one **relic** behind, a thing of its reality with its magic
still in it: a ship's bell, a basilisk's dulled eye, a ring off a dead king. A relic **bonds**
to the first person who takes it up, and works for nobody else, ever; when its holder dies it
is a curio. So relics are never bought or sold. The shops sell steel, powder and rope.
