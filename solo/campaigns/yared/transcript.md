# Transcript: Yared Tewolde

## Scene 1: The Well at Selam

We came into Selam with the sun already touching the hills, and I knew it was a mistake
before Hani said it.

"You said we'd be past the town by dark," she said. She'd counted the party again at the
crossroads, lips moving. Thirty-eight. Still thirty-eight.

"I said we'd be past it if old Beruk's knee held." It hadn't. Nothing in Ancalia holds
anymore. "The church has a door that bars and walls a metre thick. We sleep there, we leave
before the light."

Selam had been a market town once. You could still see it in the bones: a square the size
of a parade ground, the stalls fallen into grey ribs, a stone well in the middle with its
bucket chain rusted to the windlass. The barley in the fields around had gone wild, chest-high
and silver, hissing in the evening wind. Five summers untouched. I didn't like barley. Things
lie down in it.

We got the people into the church of the One. Jember stood at the door with his bow, chin up,
playing at sentry. Beruk lit a stub of candle under the cracked window of the Throne and began
to murmur the evening office, and the children settled in the pews like birds. I walked the
walls, the way I always walk them, counting doors and windows and ways a dead thing could get
in.

That's why I wasn't at the door when Saba slipped out.

Jember shouted. I came round the apse at a run and saw her: nine years old, small as a
kitten, standing at the well in the middle of the square with a tin cup, trying to work a
chain that hadn't turned in five years. She was thirsty. That was all. Children get thirsty
and forget the world is ending.

Beyond her, the barley moved against the wind.

They came out of it slowly, the way husks do, swaying, as if they'd forgotten how legs worked
and were remembering one step at a time. Nine of them. Farm folk, by the rags. One still had
a sickle in its belt. Their mouths hung open, and the Hollowing had taken the light out of
their eyes and left only hunger behind. Fifteen metres from the girl. Less.

I ran.

I'd like to say I thought of something brave. I thought: *not again*. I thought of Hadra's
gate, of the torches going out along the wall, of Ser Dawit's voice telling me to run for the
postern and bring help, and of how I ran and kept running and never brought anything back but
myself. Twenty metres of cobbles between me and the well and my legs were too slow, my lungs
too small, the world too wide, and the first husk was reaching for her hair—

And then I wasn't running in the dusk anymore.

I don't know how else to say it. The light came out of me. Not a torch, not a lamp: noon.
Noonday from my own skin, white and gold, pouring across the square so that every cobble
threw a hard black shadow and the barley turned the colour of honey. Saba's face, turned up
toward me, was lit like a window at morning. The husks flinched. Not from pain: from
surprise, I think, the dull surprise of things that had forgotten what day looked like.

The spear was in my hands. I don't remember lowering it. I drove it through the first one
under the ribs, and I was strong, strong in a way I had never been, and the shaft went
through him and into the one behind, and both of them folded like wet sacking. My free hand
swept back the third as I turned, a casual blow, the kind you'd give a door that stuck, and
its head came off its shoulders and knocked down a fourth.

I put myself between the girl and the rest. "Behind me," I said, and my voice sounded like
someone else's. "Saba. Behind me. Hold my cloak."

She held it.

They came on, because husks always come on. One got its teeth into my forearm; I felt the
jaws close and heard them grind on something that wasn't flesh anymore. Another clawed my
neck. It hurt, a little, the way hail hurts. Two of them drew a thin line of blood that
glowed at the edges like the rim of a coal. I struck and something died. I swept the back of
my hand through the air and something else died, almost without my meaning to, as if the
husks were candles and I was a draught.

I missed once. A clean thrust at the last but one, and my foot slid on a cobble and the
spear went wide. It didn't matter. My other hand caught it by the face and the light did the
rest. When the last one came at me I just put my palm on its chest, and it stopped, and lay
down, and was still.

Nine of them in the dust. The barley hissing. My own breath, loud.

I looked down. The light was still coming off me, quieter now, like the sun through a
curtain. The bite on my arm had already closed. Saba had both fists in my cloak and was
staring up with her mouth open.

"You're shining," she said.

"I know," I said. I didn't. I had no idea. Behind us the church door banged open and Hani
came out with a cleaver and Jember with his bow drawn on nothing, and the whole party
crowded in the doorway, thirty-seven pale faces lit gold.

Then Saba pulled at my cloak, and pointed past me, to the edge of the light.

Someone was standing in the barley. Tall. Still. The light reached to his knees and stopped
there, as if it didn't want to go further. I could see the shape of a Pavian surcoat, the
white chevron on the breast, the long knightly sword at the hip.

"Yared," said Ser Dawit Senai, in his own voice, the voice I had last heard on the wall of
Hadra five years ago. "There you are, boy. You ran so far."

And he stepped into the light.
