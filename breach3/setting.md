# The setting

## The city and the breaches

The city has no edge anyone has walked to. It runs on coal, gas and steam: trams, mills,
viaducts, tenements, and under all of it **the Under**, the cellars, sewers, culverts and
closed railway tunnels that nobody has a full map of. People carry pistols and bows, swords
and spells; the magic is old and the machines are new, and the Board uses both.

Several times a day, somewhere in the precinct, a piece of another world comes loose and
touches this one. That touch is a **breach**. It lasts a breath: a patch of somewhere else
is in a coal cellar or on a tram platform, and then it is gone again and has left behind
whoever was standing on it. An owlbear in a boiler house. Four wolves in a schoolyard. A
knight on a goods platform with his sword out, asking what country this is.

**They are not invaders. They are stranded.** Nothing came through a door; their ground
moved, and they were on it. A few have understood and want to go back. Some are frightened
and fight; some are hungry; some were monsters where they came from and are monsters here.
There is no way back to walk to. The only one is the **sending room** in the station's
cellar, and a thing must be brought there, walking or held.

**None of them can stay.** A stranded thing is a piece of somewhere else walking about in
the city, and the city does not hold its shape around it: clocks disagree on the street
where it is, the gaslight leans. For about a day that is all. After a day, **trouble**:
it goes berserk, or there are suddenly two of it, or its own world starts to spread over
the street it stands in, or it goes off like a boiler and takes the building with it. So
the Board's rule has no exceptions and no kind version: every one of them is **sent home or
dead**, and soon.

Nobody knows why it started. Head Office has a department studying it. The answer, if there
is one, is the long question of the campaign, and the GM keeps it in `gm-secrets.md`.

## The mast, the reading and the needle

Over the station stands the **mast**: a lattice tower as tall as a cathedral, hung with
dishes the size of house fronts (the ears), humming, lit red at night. The ears hear every
breach in the precinct as it happens, and how hard it rang. That number is the **reading**:
"it reads four". A reading says how strong the thing is and roughly where. It does not say
what it is. Nobody knows that until a pair is looking at it.

The ears take their fill twice a day, at **six in the morning and six in the evening**, and
the Reader chalks what they heard on **the board**. Readings **over ten** are not the
precinct's: the Reader telephones Head Office, and Head Office's **specialists** come in a
closed van and are gone again before anyone has had a proper look at them. The same happens
to anything stronger than the precinct's best pair.

Each automaton carries a **needle**: a brass compass the size of a saucer, coupled to his
core. Set to a reading at the station, it points at that thing and nothing else, and its
second hand shows the number. It leads the pair straight there. It can be set only at the
station, which is one reason a pair comes back in after every call.

## The Board and the precinct

The **Metropolitan Board of Wards** ("the Board") runs the masts and everything to do with
breaches. It is an office, not an order of knights: ranks, forms, auditors, a pension that
few live to draw. Each mast has a **precinct** under it, and each precinct's **station**
keeps exactly four **dungeoneers**, because that is what the Board budgets for, each paired
with an automaton. The street calls them dungeoneers; the forms call them officers.

**Why pairs, and why the lowest pair is sent.** The magic a stranded thing leaks when it
dies is absorbed by whoever is there. That is how a Probationer becomes an Inspector, and it
is what the Board is really growing. So a reading is not only a danger, it is a ration, and
the Director gives it to **the lowest pair that can handle it**: a pair can handle a reading
no higher than its own level. Sending a senior pair to a junior's reading wastes it. The
Board never tells its officers to kill. It notes who grows.

**The seats.** An officer is a **seat** before they are a person: four seats with animal
call signs, each with its own day off. The four start a few levels apart. A holder who
reaches **level 12** is **called to Head Office**, where the Board's veterans go and from
which they do not write, and the seat gets a level 1 Probationer. A dead officer's seat is
filled within days by a transfer of the same level.

Ranks go by level: Probationer (1 to 3), Constable (4 to 6), Sergeant (7 to 9), Inspector
(10 and 11). Officers are addressed by rank and spoken of by seat: "the Fox is late".

**The working day.** The Board pays for days. Pairs go out between six and six, come back
to the station after every call, eat at midday in the canteen, and drink in the evening at
the public house across the yard, where the day gets talked over. What the ears hear at six
in the evening is chalked up and waits for the morning. Two seats have Saturday off and two
have Sunday, so on those days half the station carries all of it.

**The station** has: the **Director**, who decides who is sent; the **Reader**, the
scientist who sits under the ears and chalks the readings; the **Clerk**, who keeps the
board and the forms; the **Mechanic**, who rebuilds automatons, runs the fabricator and
the sending room, and talks to machines more than to people; the **Auditor**, the Board's,
who counts; the **cage van**, steam-driven, one per pair, which holds anything up to the
size of a carthorse; and the **wall**, where the fallen are named by seat, name and day.
They are **titles, not names**: nobody in the story calls the Mechanic anything else. The
GM writes them in `people.md` as play meets them, one line each: what they are like, what
they want.

**The sending room** is in the cellar, bolted to the mast's footings: struts, dials and a
dish the size of a cartwheel over a ring of iron in the floor. Whatever stands in the ring
willing, or lies in it held, for one minute goes home. It cannot be moved, which is why
everything has to be brought in.

## Automatons

An **automaton** is a brass-and-enamel man built by the Board's mechanics from a design
Head Office does not share: every one is made and spoken of as a man, "he", never "it".
Each is bound to one officer, once, for that officer's life, and from then on he gains what
his partner gains: a level when they level, nothing when they don't. He absorbs no
breach-magic of his own, which is the point. The pair is the party.

They are **people**, with the manner of a very good servant: formal, unhurried, dry,
observant, deferential in address and immovable in opinion. They call their partner by rank
("if the Constable would step back"). They narrate tactics as service ("I have taken the
liberty of oiling the stair"). They count kills as matters attended to and things sent home
as matters resolved, and most of them, if asked, prefer the latter. They are **programmed to
cheese**: the mechanics' protocol of economy makes an automaton propose, always, the
cheapest legal way to end a fight, and he will say so before every fight unless told to
hold his tongue.

An automaton **remembers** every call he and his partner have worked, and says so. "The
Constable preferred the left-hand stair last time. I mention it only for completeness."

**The bond does not pass on.** When an officer dies, their automaton goes dark where he
stands, and no mechanic can wake that core for anyone else: it is crated and sent back to
Head Office. A wrecked automaton whose partner is alive can be rebuilt from his core, memory
and all. So a transfer arrives with a new automaton, a stranger to the seat, and when an
Inspector is called to Head Office their automaton goes with them.

**What passes on is the relics.** A core holds the pattern of every relic its pair carries,
and in the moment it goes dark it sends them home through the mast. In the station's cellar
the **fabricator**, a glass-fronted cabinet the size of a wardrobe, starts ticking, and
builds each one again a layer at a time. They are on the bench when the transfer walks in.
The Board calls this continuity of equipment. The station calls it the inheritance.

### Species: Automaton

For the automaton's character sheet, in place of an SRD species.

- **Creature Type:** Humanoid (built, not born; healed and affected as a Humanoid).
- **Size:** Medium. **Speed:** 9 m (30 ft).
- **Constructed Resilience.** Advantage on saving throws against the Poisoned condition, and
  Resistance to Poison damage. Immune to disease. Needs no food, drink or air. Doesn't sleep
  and can't be put to sleep by magic.
- **Sentry's Rest.** A Long Rest is 6 hours spent motionless and aware instead of 8 asleep.
- **Integrated Protection.** +1 to AC. Armour worn becomes part of the body: an hour to don
  or doff, and it can't be removed against the automaton's will.
- **Protocol.** Proficiency in one skill (rolled by `shift.py`) and with Tinker's Tools.
- **Bound.** Gains no XP; is always the level of his partner. His **core** (1 kg) survives
  the body's destruction and holds his memory while that partner lives; the bond ends with
  them.
- **Needle.** Carries the needle, which points at the reading it was set to at the station
  and shows its number.
- **Languages:** Common and one other.

## Names

Everyone has a plain English name. Elves have the grand ones (Percival, Cordelia), orcs the
short ones (Tom, Meg), gnomes the small ones (Pip, Nell), tieflings the virtues (Prudence,
Ernest), humans the ordinary ones (Arthur, Mary). A dungeoneer is a man or a woman, on a
coin flip, with a name to match. Automatons are all "he" and go by a surname, like a butler.
Names are used sparingly: the story speaks of seats by call sign and of the station's people
by title, and a name is mostly for the human's own pair and for the wall.

## Coins and relics

A stranded thing that is killed leaves coins worth a tenth of its XP, in the currency of
wherever it came from, which the city's moneychangers take at face because the Board vouches
for it.

Three kills in four leave something more: most often a draught or a scrap of writing with
its magic still in it, sometimes a **relic**, a thing of that other world that keeps working
here: a ship's bell, a basilisk's dulled eye, a ring off a dead king. The stronger the
reading, the better the chance of a good one. A relic **bonds** to the seat whose pair took
it up, and works for that seat's holder and automaton and nobody else, ever. When the holder
dies it is rebuilt at the station for the next one. So relics are never bought or sold. The
shops sell steel, powder and rope. A thing sent home leaves nothing: it took its belongings
with it.
